🌍 What dodge actually does

Satellites do not drive on roads. They travel at roughly 27,000 kilometres per hour, and a collision at that speed is catastrophic. Space agencies call a near miss a "close approach" or a "conjunction". dodge looks at the orbital data published openly by satellite tracking services, calculates where every object will be, and highlights the moments when two objects come too close for comfort.

The difference with dodge is that it focuses on one question: who is making the other object dodge? The project name says it plainly. When a Starlink satellite approaches a piece of debris, a defunct rocket body, or another company's spacecraft, one of them usually has to move. dodge gives you a daily screen of those events, attributed, so you can see exactly which objects were involved and how close they came.

Every screen the tool produces is signed and verifiable. That means you are not asked to simply trust a screenshot. The daily output carries a cryptographic signature that anyone can check, so the record is provable rather than just claimed.

  • The close approach list. One line per event, with the two objects named and the closest distance stated.

🧠 How It Works, Without the Jargon

Satellite tracking services publish a small file for each object describing its orbit. Think of it as a bus timetable for something moving far faster than a bus. dodge reads those timetables, runs the standard prediction maths, and compares the paths of thousands of objects over the coming day.

Where two paths come within a set safety distance, dodge records an event. It then looks up who owns each object and adds that label. The result is a ranked list of the day's closest calls, each one sourced, each one checkable.
